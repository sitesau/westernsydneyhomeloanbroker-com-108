Deposit and borrowing terms

  • Genuine savings: a deposit history most lenders want to see, usually at least 5% held or built up over time rather than arriving as a lump sum the week before application.
  • Loan-to-value ratio (LVR): the loan amount expressed as a percentage of the property value. The source material uses 80% LVR as the level where lenders mortgage insurance usually begins to apply.
  • Lenders mortgage insurance (LMI): a premium charged when borrowing above the lender's threshold, commonly 80% of value. A borrower with a 20% deposit, a guarantor option or First Home Guarantee eligibility may be able to avoid it.
  • Capitalising LMI: adding the premium to the loan balance instead of paying it at settlement. Worth asking about, because it raises the amount borrowed and the interest charged over time.
  • Guarantor: a family member who offers their own property as extra security, which can reduce the effective LVR and sometimes remove the LMI requirement.
  • First Home Guarantee: a government scheme question that a first home buyer can test with the lender, because eligibility may support a smaller deposit without standard LMI costs.

Serviceability and repayment terms

  • Serviceability: the lender's test of whether income can cover repayments after existing debts and living expenses. ASIC Moneysmart frames comparison around repayments, features, fees and long-term affordability rather than the advertised rate alone.
  • APRA 3% prudential buffer: the loading the published material says lenders add to the actual interest rate when testing repayments. It tightens what a borrower can be approved for even when their budget feels comfortable at today's rate.
  • Offset account: a transaction account linked to the loan where the balance reduces the interest charged. The source material flags it as a feature that can alter product cost, so it should be priced in rather than assumed free.
  • Fixed and variable rates: fixed repayments hold for a set period while variable repayments move with the lender's pricing. The question is which suits the stated plan, not which sounds cheaper today.
  • Interest-only repayments: a structure where early repayments cover interest only. It changes cash flow during that period and is commonly discussed in investor settings.
  • Debt-to-income context: income, existing debts and living expenses all feed the serviceability result, which is why a single calculator estimate is a weak basis for decisions near a limit.

Scenario terms: refinancing, construction and self-employed lending

  • Refinancing: reassessing an existing loan against the market to cut the rate, restructure or release equity. The source material says the review starts with the current balance, repayment type, offset balance and any fixed-rate status, plus the purpose of any equity release.
  • Bridging loan: short-term finance that covers the gap between buying one property and selling another, named in the source topic set as a scenario a local borrower may raise.
  • Construction loan and progress payments: staged funding drawn against build milestones. The source material describes progress-payment finance, so the build contract and drawdown expectations belong in the early discussion.
  • Full-doc lending: the standard self-employed path using conventional income evidence such as tax returns and financial statements.
  • Alt-doc lending: an alternative documentation route for self-employed borrowers who cannot supply standard evidence. The source material presents it as a real option, with documentation as the central question.
  • Discharge costs: fees and process steps involved in leaving a current lender, relevant to any refinance arithmetic.

Investor and specialist terms

Some expressions in the Western Sydney topic set describe strategies rather than products, and the source material treats them as specialist discussions to verify rather than assume suitable.

  • Negative gearing: the position where investment property costs, including interest, exceed rental income. It is a tax and cash flow question, not a loan approval question.
  • Debt recycling: converting non-deductible debt into deductible debt over time through structured repayments and redraw. It warrants professional advice before use.
  • Rentvesting: renting where you live while owning an investment property elsewhere, which separates the lifestyle decision from the asset decision.
  • Trust loans and SMSF loans: borrowing through a trust structure or a self-managed superannuation fund. Both change legal ownership, lender options and serviceability treatment, so they sit well outside standard scenarios.

ASIC Moneysmart's property investment material supports treating an investment property as a decision about costs, income and risk, with repayments, buffers and exit plans examined before commitment.

Who this glossary applies to

The terms matter most to a Western Sydney borrower preparing for one of the scenarios named in the source material: a first purchase, a refinance, an investment property, a construction loan, a bridging loan or a self-employed application. A first home buyer will lean most heavily on the deposit, LMI and scheme definitions. A refinancer will use the discharge, offset and equity-release language. An investor will spend more time on gearing, structure and serviceability vocabulary. Confirming that a broker holds a licence or acts as a credit representative under an Australian Credit Licence holder, and asking how the broker is paid, remains a sensible early step in any of these paths.

Common questions

What does capitalising LMI actually mean? It means the lenders mortgage insurance premium is added to the loan amount rather than paid at settlement. The source material flags this as a question worth raising because it increases the balance on which interest is charged over the life of the loan.

Does the APRA 3% buffer mean my interest rate is 3% higher? No. The published material describes the buffer as a loading used in the lender's serviceability test, applied on top of the actual rate to check whether repayments could still be met under tighter conditions. The repayments themselves are set by the loan's pricing, not the buffer.

Are specialist terms like SMSF loans and debt recycling normal broker topics? They appear in the Western Sydney topic set, but the source material treats them as specialist discussions that should be checked rather than assumed suitable. Borrowers should expect extra verification, different documentation and a clearer fee discussion before proceeding.
